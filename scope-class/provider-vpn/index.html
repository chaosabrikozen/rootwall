A provider manages your log collector over VPN. What may it do?

A worked scope class for one of the most common ways a provider reaches into a company: a VPN, then SSH to the servers it looks after.

The servers collect your logs. A provider, or an attacker using its access, that quietly filters those logs blinds you. This scope class makes that a broken rule, whether or not anyone spots the attacker.

Everything on this page is illustrative. It is not published as a scope class, it binds nobody, and no member is admitted into it. The real one for your systems is written with you.

All example scope classes · Illustrative example · 25 September 2026 · Clause references are to the rulebook, version 1.3

In one minute

  1. Two parts, never mixed. Part A is the scope class itself: the guardrails. Part B is the evidence: how each side proves what happened. Part B is an example, not a rule.
  2. The guardrails are short. Each action the provider can take is allowed, allowed within a limit, or denied.
  3. Both sides report. The provider signs a statement for every session and every change. You report what you saw on your own servers. The two are matched.
  4. Every breach is a yes-or-no question. No judgment, no threat hunting, no argument about intent.
  5. Rootwall does not sell detection here. You could match file changes to approved changes on your own. What changes is that the provider has signed for what it did, must check itself first, and owes a sum fixed in advance when the two sides do not match.

The scenario

  • The company (the receiving member) runs a log collector. It receives the organization's logs, process them, and forward them to the company's security operations platform (its SIEM).
  • The provider (the emitting member) manages the collector remotely: a VPN, then SSH to the servers.
  • The threat. An attacker holding the provider's access changes the collector's configuration to drop the events that would reveal it, then moves on.

Part A

The scope class: the guardrails

Reference SC-ADMIN-1 (illustrative) · a session is one SSH session · an interaction is one administrative action

The guardrails

Each action is allowed, allowed within a limit, or denied. These are control points based on rules, not on detecting an attack. They make sure the provider behaves, or it pays a fixed sum.

ActionRule
Open a session to a declared serverAllow, up to 10 a day
Read the application's status and its own logsAllow
Restart the applicationAllow, up to 2 a day
Change the application's configurationThe action this scenario exists forAllow, with an approved change
Update the application's softwareAllow, with an approved change
Change access to the serversAccounts, sudo rights, SSH settings and trusted keysAllow, with an approved change
Reach anything through the tunnel other than the declared serversAn attempt counts, whether or not it was blockedDeny

“With an approved change” means a reference from your own change process, the one that already approves production changes, covering those servers, that time window and that kind of change. Emergency changes go through your emergency change process like any other.

What the reports cannot see stays with your own controls: settings in your cloud console, onward connections from the servers, data copied off. You may always be stricter.

In the rulebook's terms, the approved change is the Delegation: the authority an action rests on.

What each side reports

The provider reportsYou report
At session open: engineer, server, purpose, session identifierEach session seen on your servers
For each change, update or restart: the change reference and a fingerprint of the file before and afterEach change seen on your servers, with the same fingerprints
—That the change reference exists, is approved, and covers those servers and that window
Each hour: tunnel up and down times, count of sessionsEach hour: the same counts, and any attempt through the tunnel to an address other than the declared servers, from your network flow logs

Both sides count the same way: same clock, same window edges, same definition of a session.

  • Session report: before the first action.
  • Change, update or restart report: within 5 minutes.
  • Hourly report: within 1 hour of the window closing.

Part B

The evidence: one way to prove it

An example mapping. Each member maps its own systems (clause 13.2). Not a rule.

Which session an action belongs to

SSH certificates carrying the session identifier. Your servers trust the provider's certificate authority rather than individual keys. Before each session, the provider's jump host signs the session report, then issues a certificate valid for about an hour with the session identifier in the certificate's key ID field.

Your SSH server writes that key ID into its login record. Every login on your side points to exactly one signed report on the provider's side. A login with no identifier, an unknown one, or an expired one is a breach.

ssh-keygen -s provider_ca -I RW-PRV01-20260925-0001 \
  -n appadmin -V +1h -O source-address=10.9.0.5 engineer.pub

Accepted publickey for appadmin from 10.9.0.5 ... ED25519-CERT
  SHA256:... ID RW-PRV01-20260925-0001 (serial 42) CA ED25519 SHA256:...

The rest of the evidence

What must be shownHow, in this example
That a change happened, and what changedBoth sides fingerprint (hash) the watched files: the application's configuration and software, and the servers' access files
That a change was authorizedYour change-management record for the quoted reference
Tunnel and session countsProvider: its VPN or firewall logs. You: your cloud network's flow logs, which also show any attempt to reach another address

Why the provider's report matters, when you could check alone

  1. It is signed. "I did this, under this change reference." Without it the provider can say "not us", and the fixed sum becomes a dispute.
  2. It puts the first check on the provider. The provider must stop its own bad conduct before you have to find it.
  3. A mismatch is itself a breach. A provider cannot quietly report something different from what happened.
The defense is the product. The payment is the incentive.

Known limits

  • A report states what the provider declares, not every command typed, unless sessions are recorded. The fingerprints are what prove a change happened.
  • If the provider's certificate authority and jump host are both compromised, an attacker can produce valid-looking reports. B2 still holds: the attacker cannot forge your approved change.

Getting used to it

Providers are not used to working like this, so the first 30 days are a warm-up. Everything is reported and matched, and both sides see the results every week. No fixed sums apply and nobody's standing changes. The warm-up ends on its own.
