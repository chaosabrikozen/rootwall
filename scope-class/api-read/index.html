A provider reads records through your API. What may it do?

A worked scope class for a provider that reads records through your API on behalf of a named person, such as a reporting or planning service your staff or your customers use.

Reading is the most common thing a provider does through an API, so the guardrails are about how much, on whose authority, and nothing else.

Everything on this page is illustrative. It is not published as a scope class, it binds nobody, and no member is admitted into it. The real one for your API is written with you.

In one minute

  1. The guardrails are short. Reading is allowed, within limits. Everything that changes a record, moves money or contacts someone else is denied.
  2. Every read rests on a named person’s authority. The provider reads on behalf of someone identified, and that authority lasts at most 24 hours.
  3. Both sides report. The provider and your API each send a short signed report for every session and every call. The two are matched.
  4. Every breach is a yes-or-no question. No judgment and no argument about intent.
  5. Rootwall confirms that no guardrail has been broken. The provider has signed for what it did, checks itself first, and owes a sum fixed in advance when a guardrail is broken or the two sides do not match.

The guardrails

Each action is allowed or denied, and an allowed action may carry a limit. These are control points based on rules. They make sure the provider behaves, or it pays a fixed sum.

Every read needs a delegation: the authority of a named person or organization on whose behalf the provider is reading, valid for at most 24 hours. The person is named in the report, and the provider states that it checked the authority.

Limits are counted per provider and count only the calls the provider made. They are set per hour and per day, because a limit per session could be dodged by opening more sessions.

What a scope class can and cannot say

Rootwall never sees what is inside your traffic. Reports carry the names of the fields in a call and a fingerprint of their values, never the values.
  • In a scope class: which kinds of call are allowed, how many, over what period, on whose authority, and what each side must report.
  • Not in a scope class: anything that needs a value to be read, such as a maximum field length, a date range, or what may appear in a search. Rootwall could not check it.

Those rules still exist, at your own edge. You may always apply a restriction stricter than the class, privately, without telling anyone. A refusal under that right is not a breach by anybody.

The class names kinds of call, not your endpoints. You map each of your endpoints to a kind, by what it actually does. An endpoint that reads and quietly writes maps to the write.

The same API, one step riskier

A class that also allowed creating and changing records would move everything else with it:

  • Tighter limits on the changes than on the reads, because a wrong change costs more to undo than a wrong read.
  • A shorter delegation: hours rather than a day.
  • Higher sums, for the same reason.

What does not change: the rules themselves, the reporting standard, or anything in the rulebook. A scope class sets what a provider may do. It never varies a rule.

How the real one gets written

  1. You list what your API exposes and map each endpoint to a kind. Only you know what your endpoints actually do.
  2. We set the limits together, against what your ordinary traffic looks like.
  3. The sums are set with the class, as a pre-estimate of what each kind of breach would cost you to deal with. You are the party they are paid to.
  4. It is published, and every provider admitted after you is held to it.
